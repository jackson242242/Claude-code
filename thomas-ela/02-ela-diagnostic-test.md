## PART 1 — Reading: Literature (12 points)

### Passage A: "The Last Whistle"

> Maya had practiced the corner kick a thousand times against the garage door. Left foot
> planted, right foot curling under the ball, eyes on the far post. But the garage door
> never had a goalkeeper, and it never had three thousand people holding their breath.
>
> Now the referee lifted the whistle to his lips. One minute left. The score was tied,
> 1–1, and the ball sat waiting on the white arc of the corner, as still as a held breath.
>
> Coach Reyes had told her before the match, "You don't have to be the hero, Maya. You
> just have to trust your feet." At the time she had nodded, but she hadn't really
> believed him. Heroes, she'd thought, were the ones who scored. The rest were just there.
>
> Her teammate Dani jogged over and stood beside her. "Want me to take it?" Dani asked.
> Dani had a stronger leg, everyone knew that. It would have been easy to say yes.
>
> Maya looked at the far post, then at her own two feet, scuffed and muddy and entirely
> her own. "No," she said, and the word surprised her with how steady it came out. "I've
> got this one."
>
> She didn't remember the run-up afterward, or the sound of her foot meeting the ball.
> She only remembered the way the whole stadium went silent for half a second — and then
> erupted, a wall of noise crashing down — as the ball curled past the goalkeeper's
> outstretched fingers and kissed the inside of the net.
>
> Later, holding the trophy, Dani bumped her shoulder. "Hero," Dani teased.
>
> Maya laughed. But she knew now what Coach had meant. She hadn't trusted her feet
> because she wanted to be the hero. She'd trusted them because, for the first time, she
> believed they were enough.

## PART 2 — Reading: Informational Text (12 points)

### Passage B: "How a Stadium Stays Cool"

> When tens of thousands of fans pack into a stadium on a hot summer afternoon, the air
> inside can become dangerously warm. Engineers who design modern stadiums have to solve
> a tricky problem: how do you cool an enormous space that is partly, or completely, open
> to the sky?
>
> One older solution was simply to add a roof and giant fans. But fans alone only move hot
> air around — they don't make it cooler. So engineers turned to a smarter idea borrowed
> from nature. When water evaporates, it absorbs heat from the air around it. You feel this
> yourself when sweat dries on your skin and you suddenly feel cooler. Some stadiums now
> spray a fine mist of water into the air. As the tiny droplets evaporate, they pull heat
> out of the air, dropping the temperature by several degrees.
>
> The most advanced stadiums go further. They are built with special vents near the ground
> and openings high in the roof. Because hot air naturally rises, the warm air escapes
> through the top while cooler air is drawn in from below. This constant, gentle flow
> happens almost without any machines at all. Engineers call this "passive cooling,"
> because it uses the natural behavior of air instead of huge amounts of electricity.
>
> Of course, none of these methods is free. Misting systems use water, and that water has
> to come from somewhere. In dry regions, designers must balance keeping fans comfortable
> against using a resource that is already scarce. As more major tournaments are held in
> hot climates, the question of how to cool a crowd without wasting water or energy is
> becoming one of the biggest challenges in stadium design.
