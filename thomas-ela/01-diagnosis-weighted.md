# 01 · Weighted ELA Diagnosis — Thomas Huang (Grade 4, age 10)

This is a **diagnosis with weights**: every ELA skill domain gets (a) an **Importance
weight** — how much it matters for a 10-year-old moving from "good" to "excellent" —
and (b) a **Confidence estimate** — how sure we currently are about Thomas's level in
it, *before* he takes the diagnostic test. Multiplying the gap by the weight produces a
**Priority score** that tells the study plan where to spend time.

---

## 1. What the NWEA data actually tells us

| Subject | RIT (Spring '26) | Percentile | Read as |
|--------|------------------|-----------|---------|
| Reading (ELA) | **218** | **82nd** | Well above average; strong reader |
| Math | 237 | 93rd | Excellent |

**Growth trend (Reading RIT):** 195 → 204 → 207 → … → **218** over the tracked terms —
a steady, healthy upward line, no plateau or dip.

**Three evidence-based conclusions:**

1. **Reading is solid, not deficient.** 82nd percentile means he already reads better
   than ~4 of every 5 same-grade peers. The work is *enrichment and precision*, not
   catch-up.
2. **Reading is his relative growth area.** It trails math by ~11 percentile points and
   ~19 RIT points. For a kid this capable, the highest-value move is closing his *own*
   internal gap — pulling reading up toward his math ceiling.
3. **The Family Report is sub-skill-blind.** RIT 218 is a single composite. It cannot
   say whether his strength is comprehension, vocabulary, or conventions. **This is the
   gap the diagnostic test closes** — and why the diagnosis below is weighted by
   *confidence*, not treated as fact.

**RIT 218 → reading-level interpretation:** Roughly a solid mid-Grade-4 to early-Grade-5
instructional level. The test is therefore written at **Grade 4 with a Grade-5 stretch**
so it can find his true ceiling rather than bottoming out too easily.

---

## 2. The weighting framework

Two scales, each 1–5:

- **Importance (I):** 5 = foundational / high-leverage for this age; 1 = nice-to-have.
- **Predicted confidence (C):** 5 = very likely already strong (low need); 1 = likely a
  gap (high need). This is our *pre-test bet* from the NWEA profile + typical Grade-4
  patterns.

**Need = (6 − C)** → converts confidence into "how much attention this may need."
**Priority = Importance × Need.** Higher = work on it sooner. The Importance column,
normalized, is the **weight** the study plan uses to budget time.

### Reading the table

- **Top two priorities — Writing (constructed response) and Reading inference/evidence.**
  These are *exactly* the skills a multiple-choice-heavy adaptive test like NWEA
  under-measures. A child can score 82nd percentile on MAP and still struggle to
  **explain** an answer in writing with text evidence. This is the single most common
  "hidden gap" in strong-on-paper readers, so it carries the most weight and the lowest
  predicted confidence.
- **Mid priority — Informational text & vocabulary.** As reading shifts from "learning
  to read" to "reading to learn" in Grade 4–5, nonfiction structure and academic
  vocabulary become the growth engine. Worth deliberate practice.
- **Lower priority — Literature, conventions, fluency.** Likely already strengths given
  his percentile and steady growth; the plan *maintains* these rather than drilling them.

## 4. Hypotheses to confirm or reject

The test is designed to answer these specific questions:

1. Can Thomas **cite text evidence** for an inference, not just pick the right option?
2. When he writes a response, is it **organized** (claim → evidence → explanation) or a
   single unsupported sentence?
3. Is his **academic/Tier-2 vocabulary** keeping pace with his comprehension?
4. Does he handle **informational text structure** (cause/effect, compare, sequence) as
   well as story structure?
5. Where is his true **ceiling** — does he clear the Grade-5 stretch items?

Each test section maps to one or more of these. See file 03 for the exact mapping.
