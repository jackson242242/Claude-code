### 0:00 COLD OPEN (0:00–0:50)
CUE: slug-named Great Wall ground clip, then a diagram title card "WHAT IS THE WALL MADE OF?".
VO: "Picture the Great Wall: grey brick, tall watchtowers. That wall is real — but it's only one layer of a much bigger story. A national survey measured twenty-one thousand, one hundred and ninety-six kilometres of Great Wall structures from every era. The brick-and-stone Ming wall is about eighty-eight hundred of those. A lot of what came before was built from something far humbler — soil, pounded layer by layer. And in one place in Liaoning, the Ming builders did something nobody expects: they marched the wall straight across a river."
ON-SCREEN: "21,196 km — all eras" · "Ming wall ≈ 8,850 km" · "Walls + trenches + natural barriers". (G1, G3, G4)

### 0:50 CH1 — WHAT "21,196 KM" ACTUALLY COUNTS (0:50–2:10)
CUE: S1 bar diagram: 21,196 split into labelled blocks (Ming wall 8,850; earlier walls; trenches & natural barriers — NOT to scale, no invented sub-numbers).
VO: "Start with the number. The 2012 survey counted walls, trenches and natural barriers across fifteen provinces and regions, plus over forty-three thousand heritage sites. The Ming dynasty's share is about eighty-eight hundred kilometres. That means the Great Wall is not one wall. It's a series of barriers built by different states over more than two thousand years, with different materials depending on what the ground gave them."
ON-SCREEN: "15 provinces/regions" · "43,721 heritage sites" · "Not one wall". (G1–G4)

### 2:10 CH2 — THE EARTH RECIPE: HOW RAMMED EARTH WORKS (2:10–4:40)
CUE: S2 section diagram: wooden formwork boards → soil poured → tamped → boards raised → next layer (renderer render_rammed_earth.py pattern).
VO: "Rammed earth is simple and brutal. Build a frame of wooden boards. Pour in soil. Pound it flat. Pour again, pound again. Lift the frame, and repeat. Layer by layer, a wall grows. Qin builders, on the plains, used it; in the mountains they used stone instead. It needs no kiln, no quarry, no brick factory — just soil, timber and a lot of labour. Dry, it can last for centuries. Wet, it's vulnerable — which is why it survives best in dry country."
ON-SCREEN: "1 formwork · 2 fill · 3 tamp · 4 raise · repeat". "Plains: rammed earth · Mountains: stone". (G5, G6)

### 4:40 CH3 — THE DESERT TRICK: REED LAYERS AT DUNHUANG (4:40–6:50)
CUE: S3 section: alternating gravel/sand-earth layers and reed bundles; footprints trench diagram.
VO: "Out in the Gansu desert, the Han dynasty had a problem: no good clay. So they used what the desert had — gravel, sandy soil, and bundles of reeds and tamarisk-type branches, stacked in layers. The reeds hold the structure together like reinforcement, and in the dry air some of those walls have lasted for two thousand years. The Han wall system ran, by common accounts, over ten thousand kilometres, and at Dunhuang there was even a ditch beside it, raked smooth with fine sand so footprints would show who crossed. In the ruins at the Jade Gate, Aurel Stein found wooden-strip documents dating to the first century BC."
ON-SCREEN: "Gravel + reeds + branches" · "Han wall: 'over 10,000 km' (accounts vary)" · "Sand trench: footprints = alarm". (G7–G13)

### 6:50 CH4 — THE MING UPGRADE: BRICK ON THE OUTSIDE (6:50–8:20)
CUE: S4 section: earthen/rubble core, brick shell, watchtower; label "Ming: brick & stone".
VO: "The Ming dynasty changed the recipe. Fired brick and stone instead of bare earth. General Qi Jiguang, in the late 1560s, oversaw about twelve hundred watchtowers between Shanhaiguan and Changping — hollow towers where soldiers could shelter. Many Ming sections are a brick or stone shell around a filling of earth and rubble. Brick sheds water and resists weather; the core keeps the wall massive. At the far west, Jiayu Pass in Gansu marks the end of the Ming wall."
ON-SCREEN: "Ming: brick & stone shell" · "1567–1570: ~1,200 towers" · "Jiayu Pass — Ming wall's west end". (G14–G17)

### 8:20 CH5 — THE WALL THAT CROSSES A RIVER: JIUMENKOU (8:20–10:10)
CUE: S5 side elevation: river, granite slab foundation, 8 piers, 9 arches, brick wall on top; map pin (Liaoning) next to Shanhaiguan.
VO: "Normally the wall stops at a river — the water is the barrier. At Jiumenkou, near Shanhaiguan, the Ming builders carried the wall across. The river here is roughly a hundred metres wide. They laid rectangular granite slabs across the riverbed — about seven thousand square metres, reportedly bonded with molten iron — then raised nine arched gates, each five metres wide and seven high, so floodwater could pass through. Brick wall on top. The name means 'nine water gates'. The section is about seventeen hundred metres long, built around 1381, and it's often described as the only part of the Great Wall built over water. What stands today has been restored more than once."
ON-SCREEN: "9 arches · 5 m × 7 m each" · "~100 m river span" · "Granite slab foundation ~7,000 m²". (G18–G23)

### 10:10 CLOSE (10:10–10:50)
CUE: recap diagram: formwork → reed layers → brick shell → arches.
VO: "Four tricks, one wall: tamp the earth. Add reeds in the desert. Put brick on the outside. And when a river gets in the way, build it a bridge with a wall on top. Next time: the Dujiangyan irrigation system, which has been steering a river for over two thousand years. Subscribe for 'How Ancient China Built It'."
