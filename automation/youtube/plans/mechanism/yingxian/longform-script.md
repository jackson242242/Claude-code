## 0. Honesty notes before anything else

- The brief's example title said "survived 40 earthquakes". **"40+ earthquakes" is a tourism-brochure number only**
  (dahepiao.com, smzdm.com). No heritage body or paper gives a count. The script says so out loud instead of using it.
  What IS sourced: a Yuan-dynasty quake "lasting seven days" (China Daily 2025), 1305 and 1626 "especially severe"
  (one source), "seven earthquakes from 1056 to 1103" in local records (one source). Title variants avoid a quake count.
- "No nails": Guinness and China Daily say no (metal) nails; one Chinese site says "hardly any". On screen and in VO:
  **"almost no nails" / "no metal nails in its joints"**, never "zero nails".
- "54 types of dougong": UNESCO tentative list, Structurae, Tsinghua news say 54; China Daily and 新京报 say 59.
  VO: "fifty-four kinds — some surveys count fifty-nine".
- Weight "7,400 t": one source (thepaper) + our facts file. VO says "reportedly" and it is not in any title/thumbnail.
- Footage rule: the site may be NAMED only over Pexels pxp-15955261 (slug `pagoda-of-fogong-temple-in-shanxi`) and
  Wikimedia Commons files whose title says Fogong / Yingxian / 释迦塔. Pexels has **zero** slug-named video of it
  (pivot truth table row "Yingxian Wooden Pagoda" = MAP lane). So this is a **stills + diagrams** explainer, which is the
  Ancient Architects format anyway. Look-alike Pexels photos (yao yao `ancient-wooden-pagoda-in-shanxi`, Wang Qihang
  `ancient-wooden-pagoda-in-datong-shanxi`) are NOT used — a continuous Yingxian narration over them would imply the name.
- Commons licences: CC BY 2.0/3.0 files are used freely with credit. The high-res CC BY-SA 3.0/4.0 series (2013
  "Yingxian Fogong Si Shijia Ta", 2019 dusk/aerial) is listed as OPTIONAL: CONTEXT.md tells us to avoid BY-SA music
  because the video becomes a share-alike adaptation; the same risk applies to photos, so the owner decides (§6).
- All diagrams are code-drawn (SVG → PNG), labelled "schematic / not to scale" on screen → no synthetic-content label.
  The 3 OpenAI thumbnail illustrations are clearly painted/vector, not photoreal; if one is used, the description says
  "thumbnail illustration" and we do not caption it as a photo of the site.
- No politics. No visa advice. Tibet/Xinjiang not involved.

## 1. Full script (VO + on-screen) — ~12:20

Timecodes are targets; `[V: …]` = visual (shot-list ID in §3), `[T: …]` = on-screen text, `(Fn)` = ledger fact.

### 0:00 COLD OPEN (0:00-0:52)
[V: S01 pxp-15955261 slow push-in, dark grade] [T: Yingxian, Shanxi · 1056]
In the year ten fifty-six, in a small town in northern China, carpenters finished a tower of wood sixty-seven metres tall. (F1, F2) No steel. No concrete. And in its joints, almost no nails. (F8)

[V: D04 sway animation] [T: "a quake that lasted seven days" — China Daily]
Then the earthquakes came. Records describe one that shook the ground for seven days. (F10)

[V: S03 corner-dougong photo, b/w, slow zoom] [T: 1926 · 200+ shells]
In nineteen twenty-six, two warlord armies fought through the town and more than two hundred shells hit the tower. It caught fire. It burned out. It stayed up. (F11)

[V: S01 again, colour returns] [T: tallest wooden pagoda on Earth — Guinness, 2016]
Nine hundred and seventy years later, it is still the tallest wooden pagoda on Earth. (F3) Every stone tower of its age in this region has cracked, been rebuilt, or is gone.

So how does a stack of timber beat earthquakes that shatter stone?

[V: D03 dougong build-up → D02 section]
The answer is three ideas, hidden inside the building. A joint that is designed to slip. A tower inside a tower. And four floors that visitors have never been allowed to see. (F4, F13)

I'm your China Travel Expert. Let's take it apart.

### 0:52 CH1 — WHAT IT IS (0:52-2:20)
[V: D01 map zoom China → Shanxi → Yingxian] [T: Yingxian · 85 km south of Datong]
Yingxian is a county town in Shanxi province, about eighty-five kilometres south of Datong. (F17) A thousand years ago this was the frontier of the Liao dynasty — a Khitan empire that ruled northern China and the steppe beyond it.

[V: S01 / S04 panoramio portrait, Ken Burns bottom→top]
In ten fifty-six the Liao emperor Daozong had a Buddhist pagoda raised here, by tradition on the site of his grandmother's family home. (F17) Its proper name is the Sakyamuni Pagoda of Fogong Temple — the Buddha Palace Temple. Locals just call it mùtǎ. The wooden tower.

[V: D02 section, labels appear: 67.31 m, 30.27 m, 1056]
Sixty-seven point three one metres tall. Thirty point two seven metres across at the base. (F2) That is a twenty-storey building, in timber, finished before the Normans had got round to building Durham Cathedral.

[T: 5 storeys outside · 9 levels inside]
From outside you count five storeys and six rings of eaves. Inside there are nine levels, because between every visible floor there is a low hidden one. Five bright, four dark — míng wǔ àn sì, the carpenters' phrase. (F4)

[V: S03 corner dougong photo]
And under every eave, hundreds of these: bracket sets, dòugǒng. Fifty-four different kinds on this one building — some surveys count fifty-nine. (F7) Hold that thought; they're the first secret.

[V: S01, Guinness card]
In September twenty-sixteen Guinness World Records certified it as the tallest wooden pagoda in the world. (F3) It had been that for nine and a half centuries. Nobody had asked.

### 2:20 CH2 — THE DAMAGE REPORT (2:20-4:05)
[V: D05 timeline strip 1056 → 2026, events light up]
Let's be honest about what it has been through, because the brochures aren't.

[T: "40 earthquakes"? — no verified count]
Tourism sites say "more than forty earthquakes". No heritage body, no engineering paper gives that number, so you won't hear it from me. (F10) What the records do say is this.

[T: 1056-1103 · seven quakes (local records)]
Local chronicles list seven earthquakes in the tower's first forty-seven years. (F10, single source — say "local chronicles")

[T: Yuan dynasty · "the ground shook for seven days"]
In the Yuan dynasty — the Mongol century — one earthquake is recorded as lasting seven days. The tower stood. (F10) Repairs are recorded after the quake of thirteen-oh-five. (F10, single source)

[V: D04 sway, stone tower cracking side]
This is the Shanxi rift. The province sits on a line of faults that, in fifteen fifty-six, produced the deadliest earthquake in recorded history just across the border in Shaanxi. (facts file #33, Britannica) Brick pagodas here lost their tops. This one lost nothing it couldn't replace.

[V: S03 b/w, card: 1926]
Then people tried. In nineteen twenty-six the armies of two warlords, Yan Xishan and Feng Yuxiang, fought over Yingxian. More than two hundred shells hit the pagoda. Railings and beams were smashed, the ornamental canopy at the top was blown off, and it was set alight more than once — the fires went out on their own. (F11) In nineteen forty-eight it was in the line of fire again. (F11)

[V: S02 gate photo]
Shell scars are still visible on the second and third floors. (F11) The tower is not undamaged. It is un-fallen. That is a very different, and much more interesting, thing.

### 4:05 CH3 — SECRET 1: THE JOINT THAT SLIPS (4:05-6:15)
[V: D03 dougong build-up, one piece per sentence]
Start with the dòugǒng. A column. On top of it a big cap block, the lǔdǒu. In a notch in the block, a bow-shaped arm, the gǒng. On the ends of the arm, small blocks. On the blocks, a longer arm. And so on, stepping outward and upward, until the stack is wide enough to carry the beam and the eave above it. (F7)

[T: mortise + tenon · no metal nails]
Every piece is cut to fit the piece below — mortise and tenon. There are no metal nails in these joints. Nothing is glued. The whole set is held together by its own weight and by friction. (F8)

[V: S03 corner dougong photo, push in on joint]
That sounds like a weakness. It's the point.

[V: D04 sway, left tower]
When the ground moves, a rigid joint has two options: hold, or break. A dòugǒng has a third. The blocks rotate, the arms slide a few millimetres in their notches, wood rubs on wood — and that rubbing turns the energy of the quake into heat. (F9)

[T: damping ratio > 0.1 · rises with shaking — shaking-table tests]
Engineers have put scale models of wooden pagodas and single bracket sets on shaking tables. The damping ratio comes out above zero point one — roughly double what a modern concrete frame is designed for — and the harder you shake a dòugǒng, the more it damps. The cap block and the first arm do most of the work. (F9)

[V: D03 final frame, caption]
So the first secret is a joint that is designed to give. Hundreds of them, on every level, each one a tiny shock absorber that resets itself when the shaking stops.

### 6:15 CH4 — SECRET 2: A TOWER INSIDE A TOWER (6:15-8:00)
[V: D02 section, column rings light up]
Now the plan. Each storey has two rings of columns. Eight on the inside, around the Buddha statue. Twenty-four on the outside, around the walkway. (F6) Song-dynasty builders called them the inner trough and the outer trough; engineers today call it tube-in-tube — the same idea as a modern skyscraper core. (F6)

[V: D02, beams between rings]
The two rings are tied together by beams and by those bracket sets. Push the tower sideways and the whole octagon works as one stiff ring, not as thirty-two separate sticks. (F6)

[V: D02, hidden levels turn amber, diagonal braces appear] [T: 暗层 · hidden levels · diagonal braces]
And here are the four floors nobody sees. The hidden levels are not storage. They are packed with diagonal timber braces — triangles, the same geometry as a modern truss. Each one makes a rigid belt around the tower. (F5)

[V: D02 full]
So from the ground up the structure alternates: a flexible storey that can rock, then a stiff belt that keeps it in shape, then a flexible storey, then a belt. Nine levels — five soft, four hard. (F4, F5) This is, roughly, how seismic engineers design tall buildings now. The Liao carpenters got there in ten fifty-six.

### 8:00 CH5 — SECRET 3: WHAT WOOD DOES THAT STONE CAN'T (8:00-9:05)
[V: D04 sway full]
Third secret, and it's the material itself. Stone and brick are strong in compression and useless in tension; they crack the moment you bend them. Timber bends, and comes back. (general; F9)

[V: D02 with lean indicator]
Put the three together — joints that slip, rings that stiffen, a material that flexes — and you get a tower that moves with an earthquake instead of fighting it. Micro-tremor measurements on the real building show it twisting and translating as a coupled system, exactly as the model predicts. (F9, WCEE 2008)

[T: 7,400 t (reported)]
And it is heavy — reportedly over seven thousand tonnes of larch and elm — which keeps it planted while it sways. (F21, flagged "reported")

### 9:05 CH6 — THE COST (9:05-10:50)
[V: S04 portrait, slow tilt down] [T: closed above floor 1 for more than ten years]
Here's the part the hero story leaves out. If you go today, you will not be allowed above the ground floor. The upper levels have been closed to visitors for more than ten years. (F13)

[V: D02 with lean arrow, amber on storey 2]
Because the tower is leaning. A two-thousand-and-four survey found the whole structure tilted sixty-five centimetres to the north-east and compressed by eighty-eight centimetres. (F12) On the second floor, one column — number twenty-three — was leaning more than fifty-six centimetres when it was measured in twenty-fifteen. (F12)

[T: 1930s repair removed braces · 1926 shells · 1948]
Why the second floor? The best explanation is us, not the earthquakes. A nineteen-thirties "repair" removed walls and braces on that level to open up the view; then came the shelling, then more war. (F12/F14, CGTN) The flexible storey lost part of its stiff belt.

[V: D05 timeline: 2002 four plans shelved · 2024 AI survey · 2025 debate]
Four repair plans were drawn up in two thousand and two. All four were shelved. (F14) In twenty twenty-four a Tsinghua team began mapping every member with AI-assisted scanning, because nobody could agree on how the tower was deforming. (F14) The argument now is brutal: take it apart and rebuild it, or correct it in place. Nobody wants to be the engineer who touched it and was wrong. (F14)

[V: S01 colour]
That is the honest status of the tallest wooden building in the world in twenty twenty-six: standing, leaning, and waiting.

### 10:50 CH7 — WHAT IS INSIDE (10:50-11:50)
[V: S04 / S02 gate] [T: 1933 · Liang Sicheng]
Two more things, because they change how you see it. In nineteen thirty-three the architect Liang Sicheng climbed this tower with a tape measure and drew it, storey by storey — the first modern survey of it. (F15) He wrote to his wife that it was unlike anything he had seen. (F15) His drawings are why we know the hidden levels exist.

[T: 1974 · Liao Tripitaka scrolls · tooth relics]
And in nineteen seventy-four, during repairs, workers found silver boxes sealed inside the Buddha statues: two tooth-shaped relics, and a cache of Liao-dynasty printed scriptures — among the oldest printed books in China. (F16) The tower had been a library for nine hundred years and nobody knew.

### 11:50 CLOSE (11:50-12:20)
[V: S01 push-out, title card]
So, "how did it survive?" Not by being strong. By being allowed to move, held in shape by belts you can't see, built from a material that forgives.

[T: Take it apart and rebuild — or fix it standing?]
Which leaves the question the engineers can't settle, and I'd like your answer in the comments: take it apart and rebuild it piece by piece — or fix it standing, and risk it? [T: comment: DISMANTLE or IN PLACE]

Next: the brick pagoda that the fifteen fifty-six earthquake split in half — and that a second quake closed again. Follow so you don't miss it.
