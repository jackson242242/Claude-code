## 1. Script (VO + on-screen cue). Chapters at 150 wpm.

### 0:00 COLD OPEN (0:00–0:50)
CUE: eye-level FC clip (px-40154354), then rain-sound-free; diagram title card "WHERE DOES THE RAIN GO?" (code-drawn).
VO: "July 2016. Beijing gets hit by a storm. Streets near Andingmen turn into rivers; cars float. Four kilometres away, inside a palace finished in 1420, the courtyards stay dry. Reporters asked the obvious question — how? The answer isn't a pump. There are no pumps. It's a slope, a few thousand holes, and about thirteen kilometres of tunnel you can't see. Today I'll show you how each piece works — and where it doesn't."
ON-SCREEN: "Beijing, July 2016" · "No pumps. Just geometry."  (F19, F8)

### 0:50 CH1 — A CITY BUILT ON A SLOPE (0:50–2:20)
CUE: map-style diagram (scenes.json S1: plan view, north up, spot levels 46.05 / 44.28 labelled), FC photo for "Meridian Gate" ONLY if slug-named.
VO: "The Forbidden City was built between 1406 and 1420 — about seventy-two hectares, nine hundred and eighty surviving buildings. The builders didn't flatten the ground. They tilted it. The north gate sits at about forty-six metres above sea level, the south gate at forty-four point three. Less than two metres of fall across nine hundred and sixty metres — a tenth of a percent. You'd never feel it walking. Water feels it. And to the north, Jingshan hill, by the traditional account heaped up from the earth dug for the moat, sits behind the palace like a windbreak." 
ON-SCREEN: "46.05 m → 44.28 m" · "~960 m" · "slope ≈ 0.1 %" · "Jingshan — by tradition, moat earth". Caveat line: "Dense walls weaken this self-draining effect." (F6, F7, F5, F1-3)

### 2:20 CH2 — STEP 1: RAIN LEAVES THE ROOFS AND TERRACES (2:20–4:30)
CUE: S2 section diagram — hall on a three-tier marble terrace, drops falling, spouts firing in sequence.
VO: "The great halls stand on three-tier white-marble terraces about eight metres high. Rain that lands up there can't soak into stone. So the terrace edges carry dragon-head spouts — chishou, the Palace Museum calls them. They throw the water clear of the base so it doesn't undermine the marble. Counts vary — the museum says over a thousand; travel guides say fourteen hundred and twelve. I won't give you a false-precise number. In a heavy storm, the press described it as a thousand dragons spitting water — which is a fair picture. Corner dragons, by one guide, have no spouts at all; I couldn't confirm that, so treat it as a guide's note."
ON-SCREEN: "Three-tier marble terrace ~8 m" · "Dragon spouts: 'over 1,000'" · "Count varies by source". (F15-18, F27)

### 4:30 CH3 — STEP 2: DITCHES AND CULVERTS (4:30–6:50)
CUE: S3 plan+section — open stone-lined gutters → covered culverts under paving.
VO: "From the courtyards, water finds open ditches and gratings. Those feed covered channels under the paving — about fifteen kilometres of ancient rain ditches survive, roughly thirteen of them underground. China Daily put the culverts alone at nearly thirteen kilometres. The channels are different sizes — one guide gives open ditches 0.4 to 2 metres deep; I couldn't verify the widths from the museum, so I'm not drawing them to scale. Buildings on raised platforms aren't plugged into the underground network at all — that's what the spouts are for. Two systems for two kinds of ground."
ON-SCREEN: "~15 km of ditches · ~13 km hidden" · "Diagram NOT to scale". (F8, F9, F24, F23-note)

### 6:50 CH4 — STEP 3: THE INNER GOLDEN WATER RIVER (6:50–8:30)
CUE: S4 plan view — bow-shaped river in front of the Gate of Supreme Harmony, five marble bridges; flow arrow to the moat.
VO: "All of it gathers in the Inner Golden Water River — the bow-shaped stream in front of the Gate of Supreme Harmony. Five marble bridges cross it. First built in 1417 as three, widened to five in 1465. It was decoration, a fire reserve — and the main collector. From there, water leaves through the walls into the moat — fifty-two metres wide, six metres deep — and on out of the palace. Nothing in this chain moves water uphill. That's the whole trick."
ON-SCREEN: "Inner Golden Water River" · "5 bridges · 1417 → 1465" · "Moat 52 m wide, 6 m deep". (F11-14, F4)

### 8:30 CH5 — DID IT WORK? JULY 2016 (8:30–9:50)
CUE: FC clip (px-35548691) with text cards; no storm footage unless Commons-titled "Forbidden City".
VO: "China Daily and AsiaNews both reported it: while parts of Beijing flooded, the Palace Museum stayed free of deep water in July 2016. Same storm, same city. Press called it 'ancient plumbing'. But it isn't magic — and it isn't maintenance-free. Historically the sewers were dredged once a year in spring. Today, three times a year outside winter. And on July 31st, 2023, a heavy rainstorm left ponding at the Palace Museum. An official said the risk didn't come from modern changes wrecking the old system. A good design still needs cleaning."
ON-SCREEN: "July 2016: stayed dry" · "July 31, 2023: ponding" · "Dredged 1× → 3× a year". (F19-22)

### 9:50 CLOSE (9:50–10:40)
CUE: recap diagram: slope → spouts → culverts → river → moat, one line each.
VO: "Slope. Spouts. Culverts. One river. One moat. No pumps. Six hundred years later it still works when we remember to clean it. Next time: the Great Wall — and why most of it is mud, not brick. Subscribe for the series 'How Ancient China Built It'."
