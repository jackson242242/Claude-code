## 0. Honesty notes (read first)
- **Date:** say "built around 256 BC" / "in the third century BC". Sources give 256–251 BC; never "exactly 2,280 years".
- **Never say "no dam at all" without the nuance:** there is no dam ACROSS the river; the Fish Mouth levee and the Flying Sand weir are low structures. Say "no dam blocking the river".
- **The 60/40 split is a design rule of thumb, not a measurement** — say "roughly 60% to the inner river in the dry season, roughly 40% in flood". Do NOT say "exactly" or "automatically perfect".
- **No "80% of the sediment goes to the outer river"** — the only source seen is truncated. If a sediment number is needed, use none.
- **Fire-and-water rock cutting** at Baopingkou is reported by CGTN and others but is partly tradition → say "according to tradition, workers heated the rock with fire and cracked it with cold water". Wikipedia's "eight years" for the cut → "reportedly took about eight years".
- **Bottle-Neck dimensions:** only the 20 m width has two sources. Do not use 36 m / 18 m / 18.8 m figures.
- **Irrigated area:** say "around 670,000 hectares today" (668,700 Wikipedia/IAEG; ~680,000 BJ Review). Never the Reuters "3 million hectares".
- **Li Bing's son (Erlang):** "tradition says" — historic records do not confirm him.
- **Stone statue of Li Bing (1974):** "recovered from the river in 1974"; size/weight figures are single-source → omit.
- **2008 Wenchuan earthquake:** "the Fish Mouth cracked but the system kept working" (two sources). Never "undamaged".
- Footage: site NAMED only over slug-verified clips (Pexels slug must contain "dujiangyan"). If none pass the Wednesday audit → map-format cards + code-drawn plan view; never a look-alike river. Diagrams code-drawn. No AI imagery.

## 1. Script (VO + on-screen cue). Chapters at 150 wpm.
Length note: VO below is ~720 words. The Friday run expands each chapter with ledger facts only (and may add a ~1.5-min "how to visit" close: 1 h from Chengdu by train is NOT in the ledger → re-source or skip). If it cannot reach 9 min honestly, publish at 9 min.

### 0:00 COLD OPEN (0:00–0:50)
CUE: slug-named Dujiangyan ground clip (else map card "Sichuan, China"), then diagram title "SPLIT A RIVER — WITHOUT A DAM".
VO: "Most river engineering does one thing: it builds a wall across the water. This system, in Sichuan, does the opposite. Around 256 BC, a governor named Li Bing split a mountain river in two with a long, low levee shaped like a fish's mouth. No dam blocking the river. More than two thousand years later, it is still steering water onto one of the most productive plains in China. Here's how three simple pieces do the job of a dam — and where the story gets exaggerated."
ON-SCREEN: "c. 256 BC" · "No dam across the river" · "Still in use". (D1, D2, D4)

### 0:50 CH1 — THE PROBLEM: THE MIN RIVER (0:50–2:00)
CUE: S1 map/plan: Min River leaving the mountains onto the Chengdu Plain; arrows "floods" / "droughts".
VO: "The Min River comes fast out of the mountains and spreads across the Chengdu Plain. In wet months it flooded; in dry months the plain went short. A dam would have trapped silt and stones behind it and filled up. Li Bing, the Qin state's governor of Shu, chose to guide the river instead of stopping it."
ON-SCREEN: "Min River → Chengdu Plain" · "Li Bing, Qin governor of Shu". (D3, D5)

### 2:00 CH2 — PIECE 1: THE FISH MOUTH (2:00–4:10)
CUE: S2 plan view: levee head splits the flow; inner (narrow, deep) vs outer (wide, shallow); split numbers fade in by season.
VO: "Piece one sits right in the middle of the river: the Fish Mouth, a levee whose pointed head splits the Min into two channels. The inner river is narrow and deep; it feeds irrigation. The outer river is wide and shallow; it carries floods away. In the dry season, when the water is low, the deep inner channel takes roughly sixty percent. In flood, the wide outer channel takes the bigger share, and the inner river drops to about forty. Locals call it the four-six split. Nobody turns a valve. The shape does it."
ON-SCREEN: "Inner: narrow & deep · Outer: wide & shallow" · "Dry ≈ 60% inner · Flood ≈ 40% inner". (D6–D9)

### 4:10 CH3 — PIECE 2: THE FLYING SAND WEIR (4:10–5:50)
CUE: S3 plan + side section: inner river overtops a low weir; sand and stones spill sideways into the outer river.
VO: "Piece two is a safety valve. About seven hundred metres downstream, the inner river runs past a low spillway called Feishayan — the Flying Sand Weir. When the inner river gets too high, the excess pours over it, back into the outer river. And it takes sand and stones with it, so the irrigation channel doesn't choke. It's low on purpose: low enough to spill floods, high enough to keep water in during the dry season."
ON-SCREEN: "Feishayan ≈ 710 m downstream" · "Spills floods + sediment → outer river". (D10, D11)

### 5:50 CH4 — PIECE 3: THE BOTTLE NECK (5:50–7:30)
CUE: S4 plan view: channel cut through rock; narrow 20 m throat; flood arrows bounce back.
VO: "Piece three is a gap in a mountain. To get water onto the plain, the builders cut a channel through Mount Yulei — about twenty metres wide. According to tradition, workers heated the rock with fire and cracked it with cold water; the cut reportedly took about eight years. The narrow throat, called Baopingkou, the Bottle Neck, acts like a check gate: it only lets so much water through. In a flood, the extra backs up — and spills over the Flying Sand Weir into the outer river."
ON-SCREEN: "Baopingkou ≈ 20 m wide" · "Throat = check gate" · "fire + water (by tradition)". (D12–D14)

### 7:30 CH5 — BAMBOO, STONES AND A YEARLY DIG (7:30–9:10)
CUE: S5 side view: woven bamboo basket filled with stones; wooden tripods (macha) holding a temporary barrier; dry-season dredging; stone gauge figure.
VO: "What was it built from? Long baskets of woven bamboo, filled with river stones — called zhulong — held in place by wooden tripods called macha. Cheap, local, easy to replace. Then every dry season, crews dug out the silt from the riverbed and repaired the levees and the weir. The rule was carved into local memory in six characters: dig the bed deep, keep the weir low. Under the Eastern Han, a stone statue of Li Bing stood in the river as a gauge — water above his shoulders meant flood, below his calves meant drought. That statue was recovered from the river in 1974."
ON-SCREEN: "Zhulong: bamboo baskets of stones" · "Macha: wooden tripods" · "深淘滩，低作堰 — dig deep, keep it low" · "Statue recovered 1974". (D15–D19)

### 9:10 CH6 — STILL WORKING (9:10–10:20)
CUE: S6 map: irrigated area shaded; badges "UNESCO 2000" · "ICID 2018"; small crack mark on the Fish Mouth "2008".
VO: "Today the system irrigates around six hundred and seventy thousand hectares. In 2000 it was inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage site together with Mount Qingcheng, and in 2018 the International Commission on Irrigation and Drainage added it to its heritage irrigation list. In May 2008, the Wenchuan earthquake cracked the Fish Mouth — but the system kept running, and it was repaired. The Flying Sand Weir is concrete now; the idea is the same one Li Bing used."
ON-SCREEN: "~670,000 ha" · "UNESCO 2000 (with Mt Qingcheng)" · "ICID heritage 2018" · "2008: cracked, kept working". (D20–D24)

### 10:20 CLOSE (10:20–10:50)
CUE: recap: Fish Mouth → Flying Sand Weir → Bottle Neck → yearly dig.
VO: "Split the river. Spill the excess. Pinch the intake. Dig every winter. Three pieces and a habit — that's Dujiangyan. Next time: the Zhaozhou Bridge, a stone arch that has carried traffic for fourteen centuries. Subscribe for 'How Ancient China Built It'."
