### 0:00 COLD OPEN (0:00–0:50)
CUE: slug-named Zhaozhou photo (else map card), then title card "1,400 YEARS · ONE STONE ARCH".
VO: "About fourteen hundred years ago, a stonemason in northern China built a bridge with holes in it. Four of them — two small arches tucked into each end of one long, flat stone arch. People called it reckless. It's still standing. By local count it has outlived at least ten floods, eight wars and a major earthquake. Here's how a few holes, a very flat curve and some iron did it — and which parts you see today are actually new."
ON-SCREEN: "c. 595–605" · "≈37 m single arch" · "4 'holes'". (Z1, Z3, Z9, Z16)

### 0:50 CH1 — THE PROBLEM: A FLAT ROAD OVER A FLOODING RIVER (0:50–2:10)
CUE: S1 map card Zhao County on the north–south route; river schematic dry in winter / flooding in summer.
VO: "Zhaozhou sat on a busy north–south road. The river below was almost dry in winter and spring — and flooded in summer and autumn. A bridge here had to do two things that fight each other: stay low and gentle enough for carts and horses, and stay open enough to let a flood through. The man credited with solving it is a Sui-dynasty craftsman named Li Chun."
ON-SCREEN: "Dry in winter · floods in summer" · "Li Chun, Sui dynasty". (Z2, Z4, Z5)

### 2:10 CH2 — TRICK 1: A FLATTER ARCH (2:10–3:50)
CUE: S2 elevation: semicircle arch drawn first (tall hump), then morph to the real segment (less than half a circle); rise/span chips.
VO: "Most stone arches of the time were half circles. A half circle spanning thirty-seven metres would rise more than eighteen metres — a hill, not a road. Li Chun used only a slice of a circle instead. The arch spans about thirty-seven metres but rises only about seven. That's a rise of about one fifth of the span. The road on top is gentle enough for a cart."
ON-SCREEN: "Half circle: rise = ½ span" · "Zhaozhou: rise ≈ ⅕ span" · "≈37 m span · ≈7 m rise". (Z6, Z7, Z8)
NOTE: "more than eighteen metres" = arithmetic (½ × 37), not a sourced claim; keep it as arithmetic on screen ("½ × 37 m").

### 3:50 CH3 — TRICK 2: THE HOLES (3:50–5:50)
CUE: S3 elevation: solid spandrels fill in grey, then four small arches punch through (two each side); flood water rises and passes through the holes; "lighter" weight icon.
VO: "Now the famous part. Where a normal bridge has solid stone above the ends of its arch, Li Chun left four openings — two small arches on each side. Engineers call this an open spandrel. It does two jobs. It takes hundreds of tonnes of stone off the main arch. And when the river floods high, water can pour through the openings instead of slamming into a solid wall. This bridge is the world's oldest surviving open-spandrel stone arch bridge."
ON-SCREEN: "Open spandrel = 4 small arches" · "Hundreds of tonnes lighter" · "Flood water passes through" · "World's oldest surviving". (Z9–Z12)

### 5:50 CH4 — TRICK 3: 28 ARCHES SIDE BY SIDE (5:50–7:20)
CUE: S4 cut-away / top view: the arch splits into 28 parallel thin rings; iron dovetail cramps glow at joints; one ring removed → others stay up.
VO: "From the side it looks like one arch. It's actually twenty-eight thin arches of limestone, standing side by side like slices of bread, tied together with iron dovetail cramps. That made it easier to build — and to fix. If one ring cracked, it could be repaired without pulling down the rest. And the iron joints let the arch shift a little as the ground moved, instead of snapping."
ON-SCREEN: "28 parallel rings" · "Iron dovetail cramps" · "Repair one ring at a time". (Z13–Z15)

### 7:20 CH5 — 1,400 YEARS OF TROUBLE (7:20–8:50)
CUE: S5 timeline: c.605 build → floods/wars (count chips "≥10 · ≥8, by local count") → 1933 Liang Sicheng measures it → 1950s big repair → 1966 earthquake 40 km away → today: pedestrians only.
VO: "The bridge did not survive untouched. It was repaired many times. In 1933 the architectural historian Liang Sicheng found it, measured it and made it famous again. In the 1950s it got its biggest repair ever: much of the surface stone was replaced, and modern cement was pumped into the joints. Some historians still criticise that. In 1966 a major earthquake struck about forty kilometres away; the bridge stayed up. Today it carries people on foot only — and many of the original carved stones now sit in museums."
ON-SCREEN: "1933: Liang Sicheng" · "1950s: biggest repair" · "1966: quake ≈40 km away" · "Today: pedestrians only". (Z16–Z21)

### 8:50 CH6 — WHY IT MATTERS (8:50–9:40)
CUE: S6 badges: "ASCE International Historic Civil Engineering Landmark" · "China's oldest standing bridge"; Yongtong Bridge (1130) small copy nearby.
VO: "The American Society of Civil Engineers lists it as an international historic civil engineering landmark — the same kind of honour as some of the world's famous modern works. And nearby, a smaller bridge built in 1130, the Yongtong Bridge, copies the same idea. Legend says the master builder Lu Ban put it up in one night. The truth is more interesting: someone did the maths."
ON-SCREEN: "ASCE landmark" · "Copied: Yongtong Bridge, 1130" · "Legend: Lu Ban, one night". (Z10b, Z22, Z24, Z25)
