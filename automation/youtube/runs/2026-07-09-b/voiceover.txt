Here's a number that stops you cold: ninety-five thousand dollars — one year, one American college. Now fly seventeen hours west, to Singapore, where the government just rewrote how students borrow for university. It's worth understanding why.

Starting July twenty-ninth, Singapore rolls out something called the Higher Education Student Loan. On paper it sounds dry. In practice, it quietly folds three older loan schemes into one — and it can cover up to one hundred percent of a student's subsidised tuition. Not a discount. A loan — but a patient one.

Here's the part that matters at the kitchen table. While you're still studying, the loan charges no interest at all. Zero. The clock only starts ticking after you graduate, or leave. You get up to ten years to pay it back, and the minimum is a hundred Singapore dollars a month — about the price of a few lunches. There's even a living-allowance piece, for meals and the commute between home and campus.

Now compare that to the conversation in the United States — where a private college can run forty-five thousand a year in tuition alone, and "free tuition" is still something politicians argue about rather than do.

So which country has it right? That's not really the question. The point is smaller, and sharper — two wealthy places, staring at the same problem: how does an ordinary family afford a degree? And answering it in completely different languages.
