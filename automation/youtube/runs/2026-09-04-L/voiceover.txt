Number seven: cash is not dead — carry a little. Yes, China runs on the phone. But renminbi cash is still legal tender, and by law merchants must accept it. In practice a small float — say the equivalent of fifteen to thirty US dollars, around one hundred to two hundred renminbi — quietly saves you at a rural taxi, a temple donation box, a tiny street stall, or the one machine that won't read your app. Where do you get it? Use an A-T-M inside a major bank branch. Travelers consistently report that Bank of China and I-C-B-C machines are the most reliable with foreign cards. That's your safety net — not your main plan. Which brings us to the card in your wallet.

Number six: your physical foreign card works — in fewer places than you think. A tourist assumes a Visa or Mastercard covers them the way it does back home. In China, a physical foreign card is genuinely useful in high-end hotels, international-brand restaurants, airports, and big tourist shops. But walk into a neighborhood noodle joint, a fruit stall, or hop on a city bus, and the card is dead weight — those places take a phone scan, not a swipe. So keep the card for the hotel and the big stuff. For everything else, you need the phone. And that leads to the fix.

Number five — and this is the one I teased — watch the foreign-card spending limit inside the apps. Here's the trap that catches people even after they set everything up. When you link an overseas Visa or Mastercard inside a Chinese payment app, the platform caps how much that foreign card can spend. Reported ranges in twenty twenty-six sit around three thousand to sixty-five hundred renminbi per single transaction — roughly four hundred to nine hundred US dollars — with monthly ceilings in the tens of thousands of renminbi. Those limits have actually been raised under the central bank's directives through twenty twenty-five and twenty twenty-six, which is good news, but they still exist. So if you're buying a big-ticket item — a stack of high-speed train tickets, a pricey tour — don't be shocked if one giant payment bounces. Split it, or preload a balance. Quick gut-check before we keep going: which city are you flying into first? Drop it in the comments — Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Guangzhou — and I'll reply with a payment quirk for that airport.

Number four: book smart, because the booking app you choose has its own fees. When you reserve trains and hotels from abroad, you'll likely use an English-language app. Trip.com is the common one, and it accepts foreign Visa and Mastercard — convenient, but you'll often pay a currency and card-processing buffer on top. Its Chinese sibling, Ctrip — said see-trip — leans on Alipay and tends to charge less. The takeaway isn't which brand is "best"; it's to check the final total in your home currency before you tap pay, because the same train seat can cost a few dollars more or less depending on how you paid for it.

Number three: this is the ninety-percent fix — set up the mobile payment apps that link to your foreign card. This is the single change that makes China feel easy. For the first time, foreign visitors can use the two big apps without a Chinese bank account at all. You register with your passport and a mobile number, link an international Visa, Mastercard, or J-C-B card, and verify with a passport photo and a quick live face scan. Once that's done, you pay the way locals do: open the app, show your QR code or scan theirs, done — at restaurants, convenience stores, attractions, taxis, and transport. That's the wall from the start of this video, gone.

Number two: make Alipay your main app. Between the two, Alipay is widely considered the easiest on-ramp for foreigners. Its international setup is smooth, its translation and "Tour Pass" style mini-programs are built for visitors, and acceptance is enormous — one app realistically covers around ninety percent of what you'll pay for on a trip. Set this one up first, fund it with your foreign card, and test it on something tiny, like a bottle of water at a convenience store, before you're standing at a busy counter.

And number one — the strongest tip on this list — set both apps up before you fly. Almost every payment horror story in China is really a timing story: someone tried to register, verify their passport, and pass a face scan while standing jet-lagged in an airport with a foreign SIM and shaky signal. Do it at home, on your own wifi, days before departure: install Alipay and WeChat Pay, register, link your card, and complete verification. WeChat Pay is worth adding as a backup, because a handful of merchants and social features prefer it. Land with both already working, and China becomes one of the most frictionless places on earth to spend money.

Now the bonus tip I promised. Keep that small cash float I mentioned in number seven physically separate — a hundred to two hundred renminbi tucked in a different pocket from your phone. If your phone dies, is lost, or the signal drops in a subway or a remote village, that cash is the difference between a shrug and a real problem. Redundancy is the whole game: two apps, one card, a little cash — and none of them your only way to pay.

So that's the full picture for twenty twenty-six: cash as a backstop, your foreign card for hotels and big spends, Alipay as your everyday app with WeChat Pay behind it, all set up and verified before you leave home. Get that order right and the "cashless China" thing stops being scary and starts being the best part of the trip.
