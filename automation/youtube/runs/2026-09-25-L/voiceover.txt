Number nine. Booking, and the good news. You do not need a Chinese phone number, and you do not need a Chinese bank card. Foreign travelers can now register on the official app, called one-two-three-oh-six, using just a passport. You upload a photo of the passport, and you wait for approval. That can take anywhere from a couple of hours to two business days, so do it early. If you would rather keep it simple, Trip dot com sells the exact same tickets with your passport number and a foreign card, for a small fee. Either way, remember this. Tickets go on sale exactly fifteen days before departure. Popular routes around holidays sell out fast, so book the moment that window opens.

Number eight. Read the letter in front of the train number. A G train is the flagship. Three hundred to three hundred fifty kilometers an hour, on dedicated high-speed track. A D train is still fast, around two hundred to two hundred fifty, but it stops more often and runs on older track. A C train is a short intercity hop between neighboring cities. For any long distance, aim for a G train. Same route, less time on the rails, and usually the newest carriages.

Number seven. Get to the station earlier than you think you need to. These stations are the size of airports, with real airport-style security screening. Give yourself forty-five minutes to a full hour, especially in a big city at rush hour. And here is the part that feels like magic. When it is time to board, you do not need a paper ticket. You walk up to the gate, you tap your passport on the orange reader, and it opens. The same passport you booked with is your ticket.

Number six. Luggage, because the rules surprise people. There is no checked baggage on a high-speed train. Everything comes with you, into the carriage. The official limit is around twenty kilograms per adult, and each bag should measure no more than about one hundred thirty centimeters when you add length, width, and height. In practice, nobody puts your carry-on on a scale. But big suitcases go on the racks at the very ends of each car, or on the shelf above your seat, so board a few minutes early to claim that space before it fills.

Number five. Choose your actual seat, not just the class. On the app, seats are labeled A, B, C, D, and F. There is no E. A and F are the windows. C and D are the aisles. B is the middle seat in second class, and it is the one to avoid. Traveling with someone? Grab C and D across the aisle from each other, or A and F if you both want a window and the scenery rolling by.

Number four. Food on board, so you are not stuck with a sad snack. Most G trains have a dining car. On an eight-car train it is usually car number five. On a sixteen-car train there are two, in cars five and thirteen. They sell microwaved boxed meals, instant noodles, snacks, and drinks, and a trolley rolls through the aisle too. You can pay with cash, or with the Alipay and WeChat apps. Honest tip, though. The boxed meals are just okay. What locals actually do is buy their favorite food at the station before boarding, and bring it on. Do that.

Number three. Pick your class, and this is where the money math gets fun. Take Beijing to Shanghai. Thirteen hundred kilometers, in about four and a half hours, city center to city center. Second class is a comfortable three-plus-two layout, and it costs around seventy-six US dollars. First class is a roomier two-plus-two, maybe twenty to fifty percent more. Business class reclines almost flat, and runs about two hundred forty dollars, roughly three times second class. Now compare that to flying that same route, once you add getting to a far-out airport, security, and delays. This is the tip that saves you a flight. For anything under about five hours, second class is genuinely great. Put the difference toward dinner.

Number two. Know the refund and change rules, because plans move. As of twenty twenty-six, if you buy the wrong ticket you can cancel it for free within thirty minutes of paying, as long as it is at least four hours before departure. That is limited to one order, per passenger, per day. Beyond that window there is a small cancellation fee that grows as departure gets closer. Foreigners follow the same rules as everyone else. If you booked with a verified account, you can change or refund right in the app. If not, you take your original passport to a station ticket window, and they sort it out.

And number one. The big one. Once you have ridden it, China's high-speed rail quietly replaces domestic flights for you. It is fast, it is remarkably punctual, and it drops you in the middle of the city instead of an airport an hour outside it. You show up forty-five minutes early instead of two hours, you keep your water bottle, and you watch the country go by at three hundred kilometers an hour. For most trips between big cities, the train is not the backup plan. It is the best plan.

So here is the bonus I promised. Do not wait until you land to deal with any of this. Screenshot your car and seat number for every leg, because the station signs move fast and the platforms are huge. Set that up now, and your first bullet train in China will feel effortless.
