There is a train in Shanghai that gets you from the airport to the edge of the city in about eight minutes, at over four hundred kilometers an hour, and it costs around seven dollars. Most first-time visitors walk right past it and pay four times as much to sit in traffic. That is exactly the kind of thing this guide fixes. Stay to the end and I will give you the one app that quietly solves almost every transport problem in China.

Here is the good news. Getting around China in twenty twenty-six is genuinely easier than getting around most of the countries you have already visited. The trains are fast, the metros are cheap and spotless, and the apps now speak English and take your foreign card. The catch is that almost none of it works the way you expect on day one, and nobody explains the order to set it up in. So let us walk through it, piece by piece, the way a friend who lives there would.

Number six: the high-speed trains. This is the backbone of the whole country. China now has more than fifty thousand kilometers of high-speed rail, more than the rest of the world combined, running at up to three hundred and fifty kilometers an hour and linking more than fifty cities. For a traveler, that means you can usually skip domestic flights entirely. Beijing to Shee-ahn, Shee-ahn to Chung-doo, then on to Shanghai — each leg is just a few smooth hours, city center to city center, with no airport security marathon. Ride second class. Travelers consistently report it is clean, fast, and a fraction of the price of first class, and on most routes the difference is just a slightly wider seat. Book through the official app, called one-two-three-oh-six, or through Trip dot com, which adds a small service fee of a couple of dollars but runs fully in English and takes international cards. Either way, book a few days ahead — the popular seats on the best routes do sell out. To put real numbers on it: Beijing to Shanghai is about thirteen hundred kilometers, and the fast train covers it in roughly four and a half hours, with a second-class seat costing somewhere around five hundred and fifty yuan — call it seventy-five to eighty dollars. Compare that to the airport time on both ends and the train usually wins. And on the longest routes there are even overnight high-speed sleepers, so you can lie down in one city and wake up in another with a full day still ahead of you.

Number five: the city metros. This is your daily workhorse. Forty-something Chinese cities have a subway now, and they are modern, air-conditioned, and almost absurdly cheap. In Shanghai the fare is distance-based: about three yuan — under fifty cents — for the first few stops, rising gently the farther you go. Here is the part that changed recently: since twenty twenty-five the entire Shanghai Metro accepts a tapped Visa or Mastercard straight at the gate, as long as your card has no-pin contactless switched on. No ticket machine, no local card, just tap and walk. Every station has English signage and English announcements, and the maps are color-coded by line, so this is the one part of Chinese transport you can genuinely wing. Two small heads-up: every metro entrance has an airport-style bag scanner, so leave a couple of minutes for it and don't carry anything you'd carry through security; and let people off the train before you push on — it keeps the whole system moving.

Number four: taxis, the easy way. Forget flagging a cab and fighting a language barrier. Download Dee-Dee — it is China's version of a ride app, and it runs in English. You type your destination in English, it sends the address to the driver in Chinese automatically, and it can charge your overseas credit card directly. You never speak a word, you see the price before you commit, and you avoid the small handful of drivers who might otherwise take the scenic route. A taxi from the airport into central Shanghai usually runs about one hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty yuan, call it twenty to thirty-five dollars — fine when you have heavy bags or arrive late.

Which brings me to the mid-trip re-hook, because number three is where the smart money is. If you are flying into Shanghai Pudong, here is the move most people miss. Instead of that thirty-dollar taxi crawl, take the Mag-lev — the magnetic-levitation train from Pudong Airport to Long-yang Road station. It costs about fifty yuan, roughly seven dollars, and it covers thirty kilometers in eight minutes at up to four hundred and thirty kilometers an hour. It is not just cheaper. It is one of the fastest passenger trains on Earth, and riding it is a genuine bucket-list moment before you have even left the airport. Quick question while you are here: which city are you flying into first? Tell me in the comments and I will point you to the exact transfer.

Number two: the apps to set up before you fly. This is the single biggest mistake I see, so do it at home, on your own wi-fi, while your bank's verification texts still reach your phone. Set up Ally-pay or Wee-chat Pay and link your foreign card — this is how you pay for almost everything, including most of the transport we just covered. Install Dee-Dee for taxis. Install one-two-three-oh-six or Trip dot com for trains. And download an offline map and a translation app, because your usual map service will not load reliably once you land. Do this on the plane and you walk out of arrivals already able to pay, ride, and navigate. Do it after you land, and you are stuck at the airport trying to make a foreign SIM connect to a network that blocks half your apps.

And number one, the thing that ties it all together: get online before you clear customs. Nothing above works without a connection, and a normal foreign SIM will hit China's firewall. The clean fix is a travel eSIM that routes through an overseas network — you activate it before departure, and the moment you land your apps simply work, no separate workaround needed. Sort your connection first, and payment, trains, taxis, and maps all fall into place behind it. Skip it, and even the best-laid plan stalls at gate one.

So that is the order that actually works: get online, set up your payment and transport apps, then lean on trains between cities, the metro within them, Dee-Dee when you are tired, and the Mag-lev for a grin on arrival.

One more option worth knowing, for the places the fast trains don't reach. A handful of the most beautiful spots — smaller mountain towns, some national parks — sit off the high-speed network. There, a long-distance coach or a slower regular train fills the gap. They take longer and the booking is less English-friendly, so this is exactly where Dee-Dee, a hired car, or a local day-tour earns its keep. Plan those legs first, because they shape your whole route.

And the bonus tip I promised. Keep about two hundred yuan in cash in your bag anyway. Ninety-nine percent of the time you will pay by phone, but a tiny rural noodle stall, a temple donation box, or a vending machine in a small town may still want paper money — and that one backup twenty-dollar float saves the day more often than you would think.
