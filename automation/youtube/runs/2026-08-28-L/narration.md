=== SECTION: 03 Number 8 — Two systems people confuse ===
Number eight: there is no single "visa-free China." There are two completely separate systems, and mixing them up is the root of most mistakes on this list. System one is thirty-day visa-free entry — a full tourist stay, no onward ticket required, you come and you go as you please for a month. System two is the two-hundred-and-forty-hour transit — that's ten days, and it sounds similar, but it's transit, which means it comes with real strings attached. Which one applies to you depends entirely on the passport in your pocket. Picture two travelers standing in the same immigration line in Shanghai: a German tourist and an American tourist. The German can walk in for thirty days on nothing but a passport. The American cannot — they're on the transit system, with all its conditions. Same line, same officer, completely different rules. So before you book anything, the first question is never "where do I want to go" — it's "which system does my country fall under." Everything downstream depends on that one answer.

=== SECTION: 04 Number 7 — 30-day visa-free: who qualifies ===
Number seven: the thirty-day deal, and who actually gets it. As of twenty twenty-six, ordinary passport holders from roughly fifty countries can enter mainland China and stay up to thirty days with no visa and no application at all — for tourism, business, visiting friends and family, or transit. Break that down and it's about thirty-five European countries, seven in Asia, six in the Americas, and two in Oceania. Some of the biggest additions are recent: the United Kingdom and Canada were both added on February seventeenth, twenty twenty-six. And the whole scheme currently runs through December thirty-first, twenty twenty-six — a deadline worth circling. Now the dollar math, because I always give you the dollar math. A paid Chinese tourist visa can easily run over a hundred U.S. dollars once you add service and processing fees, plus a mailed passport and a week or two of waiting. If your country is on this list, you skip every bit of that — no forms, no fee, no waiting. For a French family of four, that's several hundred dollars and a stack of paperwork that simply evaporates. But here's the boundary you cannot cross: thirty-day visa-free is for tourism and short business only. You may not work, you may not enrol in study, and you may not do paid journalism. Those still require a proper, applied-for visa — every time.

=== SECTION: 05 Number 6 — 240-hour transit explained ===
Number six: what if your passport isn't on that thirty-day list? If you hold a United States passport, for example, it isn't — and this is your route instead. The two-hundred-and-forty-hour visa-free transit covers around fifty-five countries. You can stay up to ten full days, and it now reaches twenty-four provinces and regions through more than sixty entry ports, from Beijing and Shanghai down to Guangzhou and beyond. But the catch is right there in the name: transit. Officially, you are passing through China on your way to a third country — and that means you need a booked onward ticket out. Let's do the dollar math on that. Yes, the onward ticket is a genuine cost you can't avoid — but a short hop to Seoul, Hong Kong, or Bangkok can run just sixty to ninety dollars. Set that against a multi-year Chinese tourist visa, which for some nationalities costs well over a hundred and fifty dollars and takes weeks to get, and the transit route is the bargain of the two — ten days in China for the price of a budget flight. The only condition is that you follow the next four rules exactly, because this is where good trips go wrong.

=== SECTION: 06 Number 5 — The onward-ticket mistake ===
Number five — and now we're into the mistakes that don't just annoy you, they void your entry. This first one is the simplest and the most common: no booked onward ticket, no transit. Full stop. Travelers show up at check-in with a flight they've searched online but never actually purchased, figuring they'll sort it out after they land. Search results do not count. A screenshot of Google Flights does not count. You need a confirmed ticket — a real seat, on a real date — leaving mainland China for a third country, and you need it in hand before you board your inbound flight. Airline staff at your departure city often check for it before they'll even let you on the plane, and Chinese immigration will check it again on arrival. The fix costs you nothing but ten minutes and a little discipline: book the onward leg first, pay for it, screenshot the confirmation, and have it open and ready at the counter. Do that, and this mistake disappears entirely.

=== SECTION: 07 Number 4 — The third-country trap ===
Number four — the one I promised you at the very start, the mistake that gets people turned around after they've already crossed an ocean. On the transit system, your onward ticket has to go to a different country or region than the one you arrived from. Read that twice. Fly in from Tokyo, and you cannot simply book a flight back to Tokyo — that's not transit, that's a round trip, and it breaks the rule instantly. You need a genuine third destination. This is where people get clever and get burned: they book China and back, assume it counts, and find out at the border that it doesn't. Now, one very useful piece of good news is hiding in this rule. For transit purposes, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan count as separate regions — so a mainland-China-to-Hong-Kong onward ticket can absolutely qualify as that third destination. Get the direction wrong, though, and no amount of arguing at the desk will save you; the officer isn't being difficult, the rule is simply binary. So map your route as A, to China, to C — never A, to China, and back to A.

=== SECTION: 09 Number 3 — Wrong region or wrong port ===
Number three: staying inside the lines. Transit is not a nationwide pass — each entry port comes with a permitted region, and you are expected to stay within it for your whole visit. Enter through the Shanghai area, and you're generally free to roam the Yangtze River Delta — Shanghai, and the neighbouring provinces around it. Enter somewhere else, and your allowed zone is drawn differently. Plan a spontaneous side trip that crosses out of your zone, and you've quietly broken the terms of your own entry. And it goes further: not every airport in China is even eligible for transit. Some, like Lhasa in Tibet and Urumchi in the far west, are simply not on the transit list at all — land there expecting visa-free, and you'll have a problem. The fix is pure homework: before you book a single hotel, confirm that your entry port and every city on your itinerary sit inside the same permitted region. Ten minutes with the official list saves your whole trip.

=== SECTION: 10 Number 2 — The midnight clock and what's banned ===
Number two: the clock myth — and for once, the rule is actually in your favour. Your two-hundred-and-forty hours do not start the moment your wheels touch the runway. The count officially begins at midnight on the day after you clear immigration. So if you land on a Monday afternoon, that entire Monday doesn't burn against your ten days — the clock only starts ticking at midnight. That's most of a bonus day that a lot of travelers never realize they've been handed. Use it. What the clock does not buy you, though, is any change to what you're allowed to do: visa-free means tourism and short business, and that's the whole list. No paid work, no enrolling in a course, no journalism on assignment. Treat the ten days as generous — because they are — but count them from the correct midnight, and whatever you do, never schedule your outbound flight for the very last hour of the very last day. Give yourself a buffer.
