# Slot b (Longform, 16:9) — Why the SATs Are Suddenly Mandatory Again

**Pillar:** education-trends · **publishAt:** 2026-07-08T17:00:00Z
**Target length:** ~1,100 spoken words (≈6.5 min)

## Hook
A few years ago, the SAT looked finished. Elite colleges dropped it, and headlines
called it the end of the standardized test. In 2026, that story completely
reversed — and the schools leading the reversal are the same ones that killed it
first. Here's what actually happened, and why it's more complicated than either
side will tell you.

## Section 1 — The reversal is real
During the pandemic, almost every selective U.S. college went "test-optional" —
you could apply without an SAT or ACT score. Many people assumed it was permanent.
It wasn't. Harvard reinstated a testing requirement in April 2024, starting with
students applying for fall 2025. Dartmouth, Brown, Yale, Cornell, and Penn followed.
For the 2026-27 cycle, six of the eight Ivy League schools — Harvard, Yale,
Dartmouth, Brown, Cornell, and Penn — require a test score again, alongside MIT,
Caltech, and Stanford. Yale even ran a brief "test-flexible" experiment, accepting
AP or IB scores instead, then ended it in 2026 and went back to requiring the SAT
or ACT outright. Outside the Ivies, UT Austin reinstated testing, Ohio State
brought back a requirement for 2026, and Johns Hopkins announced it's phasing
test-optional out. Only Columbia and Princeton are still test-optional among the
Ivies — and both are in their final test-optional cycle, with requirements
returning for 2027-28.

## Section 2 — Why the schools say they changed their minds
The colleges didn't reverse course because they missed the test. They argue the
test-optional experiment didn't deliver what it promised. The original pitch was
that dropping the SAT would widen access for disadvantaged students. Several
schools — Brown, Cornell, Yale, and Penn among them — concluded it hadn't. Most
strikingly, Dartmouth pointed to its own faculty study arguing that test scores
could actually *help* less-advantaged applicants — first-generation and low-income
students — by giving admissions officers a signal that stood out against a weaker
high-school profile. The other argument is grade inflation: when nearly everyone
has an A average, a transcript tells you less than it used to, and a standardized
score becomes a tiebreaker.

## Section 3 — The catch nobody puts on the brochure
Here's the part that complicates the clean comeback story: the evidence is
genuinely contested. In March 2026, a widely-reported study of a large public
university system found that high-school GPA was actually the *stronger* predictor
of college success — better than test scores at forecasting both first-year GPA
and whether a student stays enrolled. So depending on which research you read, and
which type of school you're talking about, you can build a case either way. Elite,
low-acceptance schools argue scores add signal at the very top of the applicant
pool. Other researchers argue grades still win. Both things can be partly true —
which is exactly why this remains a real debate and not a settled question.

## Section 4 — The numbers that made this year's headlines
This all landed during the most competitive admissions year on record. On Ivy Day
— March 26, 2026 — the Class of 2030 results came out, and the rates were brutal.
Columbia admitted 4.23% of applicants, down from 4.94% the year before. Yale
admitted just 2,328 students out of 54,919 applications — a 4.24% rate the
university called the second-lowest in its history. Brown came in around 5.35%.
And notably, Harvard and Princeton declined to release official numbers for the
second year running, saying the obsession with acceptance rates is unhealthy. When
rates are that low, schools want every extra data point they can get — and a test
score is one more lever to sort tens of thousands of near-identical applications.

## Section 5 — What this actually means for you
Now the reality check, because the headlines make this sound bigger than it is. The
test comeback is concentrated at the most selective schools. Zoom out, and
according to FairTest, more than 90% of ranked four-year U.S. colleges — over two
thousand institutions — will *still* not require a score for fall 2026. Big names
like Chicago, USC, Vanderbilt, Emory, and liberal-arts colleges like Amherst,
Bowdoin, and Williams remain test-optional. So the honest takeaway is this: if
you're aiming at the Ivies, MIT, Stanford, or a handful of flagships, plan to test
— that door is closing. But for the overwhelming majority of American colleges, a
strong score is an asset, not a requirement. Know the specific policy for every
school on your list, because in 2026 they genuinely disagree with each other.

## Recap
So: the SAT isn't universally "back." A cluster of the most selective schools
reversed their test-optional policies, citing equity data and grade inflation; the
research on whether scores or grades predict success better is still contested; and
meanwhile the vast majority of colleges remain test-optional. The right move isn't
to panic — it's to check each school's policy and prepare accordingly.
