Let's count down.

Mistake number fifteen: landing with no mobile payment set up. In China, cash is now the exception, not the rule. Street stalls, taxis, temples, vending machines — almost everything runs on a QR code. In one recent traveler survey, sixty-eight percent said payment was their single biggest frustration on a first trip. The fix is easy and free. Before you fly, install Alipay or WeChat, and link an international Visa or Mastercard right inside the app. Foreign cards work now without a Chinese bank account. Set it up at home, and you'll be paying like a local from the airport train.

Mistake number fourteen: assuming your usual apps will just work. On mainland networks, a lot of Western services simply don't load — Google, Gmail, Google Maps, Instagram, WhatsApp, and more. Travelers commonly set up a VPN before arrival, and lean on the apps that do work locally, like WeChat, Alipay, and Amap for maps. Two things to remember: install anything you need before you land, because setting it up afterward is much harder, and app availability and rules change, so always check current, official guidance close to your trip. Connectivity is a setup task, not an in-flight scramble.

Mistake number thirteen: trusting Google Maps to navigate. Even with a workaround, Google Maps is often outdated or misaligned inside China. The maps locals actually use are Amap — that's Gao-De — and Baidu Maps, though those are mostly in Chinese. Apple Maps works reliably in China if you're on an iPhone, and it's in English. Whatever you choose, download an offline map of your city before you arrive, and save your key spots in advance. Getting lost with a dead map on day one is a classic, avoidable first-timer moment.

Mistake number twelve: carrying only a credit card and zero cash. Yes, China runs on phones — but keep a little paper money as backup. A small vendor's QR scanner can fail, a rural temple may prefer cash, and your phone can always run out of battery at the worst time. Pull a few hundred yuan from an airport ATM when you land. A hundred yuan is roughly fourteen US dollars. You probably won't spend much of it, but that cushion turns a stressful moment into a non-event.

Mistake number eleven: not saving your hotel address in Chinese. Your driver may not read a single word of English, and the pinyin spelling won't always help. Before you leave the hotel, screenshot its name and address in Chinese characters, and save it in your photos or notes. Show the screen, and you're home. Do the same for the day's big destinations. This one tiny habit prevents more taxi headaches than any phrase you could memorize.

Mistake number ten: trying to flag down taxis on the street. You can, but the smoother way is DiDi — China's version of Uber, with over five hundred million users. It has an English version, and in 2026 the most stable setup for tourists is opening DiDi as a mini-program inside Alipay, paying with your linked foreign card. One heads-up: at airports, train stations, and big malls, pickups happen only at marked designated zones — the green dots on the map — so walk to one. And your driver will ask for the last four digits of your phone number to confirm it's you.

Mistake number nine: underestimating how big China really is. China is roughly the size of the United States or of all of Europe. Beijing to Guilin is not a casual day trip. The good news: the high-speed rail network is superb. Beijing to Shanghai takes about four hours and eighteen minutes, with more than fifty trains a day, and a second-class seat runs roughly three hundred to six hundred fifty yuan — about twenty-five to a hundred US dollars. Book on Trip.com or Klook in English, or the official twelve-three-oh-six. Just don't try to see five cities in five days.

Mistake number eight: showing up at famous sites without a reservation. Many of China's top attractions cap daily tickets and sell out fast. The Forbidden City is the classic example — you cannot buy at the gate, every visitor must book online in advance, popular dates sell out within minutes, and you need your passport number to book. Admission is only sixty yuan in high season, about eight and a half dollars, so the barrier isn't money — it's planning. Same story for the Mogao Caves in Dunhuang. Book the big-ticket sites the moment your dates are set.

Mistake number seven: traveling during Golden Week without knowing it. Every year, October first through seventh is a national holiday, and hundreds of millions of people travel at once. Trains sell out, hotel prices spike, and the great sites turn into oceans of people. Chinese New Year, in late January or February, is similar. If your dates are flexible, steer around these windows. If they're not, book absolutely everything — trains, hotels, marquee attractions — as far ahead as you possibly can. Forewarned is half the battle.

Mistake number six: expecting English everywhere. In top international hotels and major sights, you'll be fine. But in local restaurants, small shops, and smaller cities, English is rare. Download an offline translation app before you go, and learn a few words — nee-how for hello, shieh-shieh for thank you. A translation app with a camera mode that reads menus and signs is worth its weight in gold. A little effort here doesn't just prevent problems — it visibly delights the people you meet.

Mistake number five: drinking straight from the tap. Tap water in China is not meant to be drunk unboiled. The easy fix is already in your room: nearly every hotel provides a free electric kettle and a bottle or two of water. Boil tap water and let it cool, or buy bottled water for a couple of yuan — a few cents. Carry a bottle when you're out. It's a small thing, but an upset stomach can wreck two days of a short trip.

Mistake number four: tipping out of habit. In most of China, tipping simply isn't customary. Restaurants, taxis, and hotels don't expect it, and trying to tip can even cause confusion. The clear exception is a private tour guide or driver, where a tip is optional and appreciated for good service. So relax — that ten to twenty percent reflex from home doesn't apply here, and no one is judging you for skipping it. Your budget quietly thanks you.

Mistake number three: not carrying your passport, everywhere. This is the one that gets people turned away. In China, your passport is your master key. You need it to check into a hotel — foreign guests are registered on arrival — to enter train stations and board high-speed trains, to pick up pre-booked attraction tickets, and to buy a local SIM card. A photo on your phone is not enough. Keep the physical passport on you, protect it, and photograph the ID page separately as a backup.

Mistake number two: assuming visa-free entry means no rules. China has expanded visa-free and transit options for many nationalities in 2026, and that's fantastic — but every scheme has conditions. Depending on your passport and route, you may need a confirmed onward ticket, you may be limited to certain entry ports, and some activities aren't allowed on a visa-free entry. Get one detail wrong, and you can be denied boarding before you even leave home. So confirm your exact category, your dates, and your ports against official sources — your nearest Chinese embassy and the National Immigration Administration — and do it close to departure, because rules move.

And mistake number one, the big one: over-planning your trip into a checklist and never actually wandering. First-timers pack the itinerary — one city a day, monument to monument, exhausted by lunch. But the China people fall in love with isn't on the ticket stub. It's the six a.m. market where breakfast is cooked in front of you, the side street of a heritage town after the tour buses leave, the night market where nobody speaks your language and everyone feeds you anyway. Build your trip around a couple of anchors a day, then leave real space to get lost on purpose. That's where the trip becomes yours.

Now, as promised, your bonus tip: prepare an offline kit before you land. Download an offline translator and an offline map, screenshot your hotel address in Chinese, save a photo of the metro map, and set up an eSIM or plan so you're connected the second you step off the plane. Do that, and your very first hour in China — usually the most stressful — becomes the easy part.
