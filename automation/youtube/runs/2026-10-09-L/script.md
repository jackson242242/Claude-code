## NARRATION (English only, ~1,350 words, ~9 min at documentary pace)

### [00:00] COLD OPEN / HOOK
China's national parks are some of the most jaw-dropping places on Earth — rainbow
sandstone, 3,000-foot quartz pillars, lakes so clear they look fake. But here's the
part nobody tells first-time visitors: at the best ones, you can't just show up.
Turn up at the gate without the right booking, on the wrong day, in the wrong season,
and you'll either be turned away or stare at fog. I'm your China Travel Expert, and in
the next few minutes I'll walk you through exactly how tickets, booking and timing work
at China's top nature parks in 2026 — so you spend your trip inside the view, not
arguing at a ticket window.

### [00:35] CHAPTER 1 — THE ONE RULE THAT CHANGES EVERYTHING: REAL-NAME BOOKING
Across China, major parks and museums now run on real-name, ID-linked tickets. For
foreign visitors that means one thing: when you book online, you enter your passport
number, and the name on the ticket has to match your passport exactly. Then you carry
that same physical passport to the gate for check-in. Most big parks have quietly
retired walk-up paper windows, so "I'll buy it there" is no longer a plan.
The catch for travellers is that a lot of the official mini-programs run in Chinese
only, and some don't accept foreign passports at all. The reliable workaround most
foreign visitors use is Trip.com, which takes foreign passport details directly.
Expert tip number one: the day you lock your dates, book your park tickets. Not the
week of. The best parks sell their daily quota, and the booking window is short.

### [01:45] CHAPTER 2 — JIUZHAIGOU: THE PARK YOU MUST BOOK AHEAD
If you only remember one park's rules, make it Jiuzhaigou, in the mountains of northern
Sichuan. This is the fairytale one — more than a hundred terraced lakes in impossible
blues and greens, stacked waterfalls, snow peaks behind. It is also the strictest.
Jiuzhaigou caps daily visitors at roughly forty thousand — the exact number isn't
officially published, but every guide puts it in the tens of thousands — and tickets
are released on real-name booking, usually one to fifteen days ahead. There is no
walk-up option. Peak-season entry runs about two hundred and eighty yuan, that's
one hundred and ninety for admission plus a ninety-yuan mandatory shuttle bus, because
you ride the park's buses between the valleys. And there's a hard final-entry cutoff
reported around two in the afternoon — arrive later and the day is gone.
Now timing. Jiuzhaigou's autumn is the famous season, and it is savage in how short it
is. The gold-and-red foliage over those blue lakes peaks for roughly two to three
weeks, most reliably from about October eighteenth to the thirty-first. Come a week
early and it's still green; a week late and the branches are bare. And avoid the first
week of October — the national Golden Week holiday — when the cap fills almost
instantly and the boardwalks are shoulder to shoulder. Expert tip: target late
October, book two weeks out, and start your day at opening, not after lunch.

### [03:40] CHAPTER 3 — ZHANGJIAJIE: THE "AVATAR" MOUNTAINS, AND HOW TO RIDE THEM
Head to Hunan province for the other must-see: Zhangjiajie, the forest of towering
sandstone pillars that helped inspire the floating mountains in Avatar. The good news:
Zhangjiajie is friendlier than Jiuzhaigou. I couldn't find a foreigner-specific daily
cap for 2026; the main forest-park pass runs about two hundred and twenty-eight yuan
and is valid for four days, which is generous because this park is huge and you'll want
the time. You book the main entry online — again, Trip.com for foreign passports — but
the cable cars and lifts inside can usually still be bought on-site with Alipay or cash.
The signature ride is the Bailong Elevator: a glass lift bolted to a sheer cliff that
Guinness recognised in 2015 as the world's tallest outdoor elevator, around three
hundred and twenty-six metres. It's about seventy-two yuan one way and climbs the cliff
in a little over a minute — versus a long stair climb to the same viewpoints. Expert
tip: ride the elevator up for the instant reveal, then walk the clifftop trails down.
You get the big moment fast, the quiet views after, and you skip the worst queue.

### [05:30] CHAPTER 4 — TIMING THE REST: SEASON IS A TICKET TOO
Two more timing rules that save trips.
First, the "clear-view" parks. Some of China's best scenery hides in cloud. Huangshan,
the misty granite peaks, and Changbai Mountain's crater lake on the northeastern border
are both famous for showing up only on a fraction of days — Changbai's Heaven Lake is
often said to be fully clear only around fifty days a year. If a crater lake or a sea of
clouds is the whole reason you're going, build in a buffer: give it two mornings, not
one, and lean toward autumn, which tends to be clearer and drier than midsummer.
Second, the seasonal-access parks. Some of the most spectacular drives and high parks
simply close for winter. Xinjiang's Duku Highway — five hundred and sixty-one kilometres
from desert to glacier — is only open roughly June to October. Miss the window and you
wait a year. So sequence your trip around what's actually open: deserts and grasslands
in summer, foliage parks in late October, and save the indoor-heavy cities for winter.

### [07:10] CHAPTER 5 — THE BOOKING CHECKLIST
Let's make this concrete. Before you fly:
One — passport ready and valid; every park booking is tied to it.
Two — book the strict parks first, Jiuzhaigou above all, one to two weeks out, on
Trip.com for foreign passports.
Three — match the name on every ticket to your passport, exactly.
Four — check each park's season and daily cutoff; late October for foliage, summer for
the high roads, and never the first week of October if you can help it.
Five — bring the original passport to every gate; screenshots don't clear check-in.
Do those five things and the gate becomes a formality instead of a gamble.

### [08:05] CLOSE / CTA
That's the system: real-name booking, the strict parks locked early, and timing treated
as seriously as the ticket itself. Nail those and China's nature opens up — rainbow
hills, pillar forests, mirror lakes, all of it. I'm mapping all of China — real prices,
real rules, zero tourist traps — and route guides and trip-planning drops are coming.
Subscribe, check the channel page, and tell me in the comments: which of these parks is
first on your list? I'll see you on the next one.
