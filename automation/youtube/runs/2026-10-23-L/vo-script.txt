Tickets are online only, released seven days ahead, and tied to your passport. There are no same-day sales at the gate. The museum is closed on Mondays, except on public holidays. Standard admission is sixty yuan through October the thirty-first, and forty yuan from November the first, with the Treasure Gallery and the Clock Gallery each costing ten yuan more. That's the headline. Now, here is how to get the ticket, how the route works, and what you should not miss inside.

First, booking. The Palace Museum uses a real-name system, so every ticket is linked to one person's passport, and one ID gets one admission per day. Tickets are released exactly seven days in advance, in the evening, Beijing time. Popular dates, weekends, holidays, and the summer peak can sell out within minutes of release. So set a reminder, have your passport details ready, and book the moment the window opens. If you want the Treasure Gallery, with its jade and gold from the imperial collection, or the Clock Gallery, with its mechanical timepieces, add them when you buy your main ticket. They are ten yuan each, and far easier to add up front than on the day. Bring the physical passport you booked with, because that is what gets scanned at the entrance.

Now, is it good value? Strikingly so. At sixty yuan in peak season, the Forbidden City costs under eight euros to enter. Compare that to the Palace of Versailles, near Paris. Its Passport ticket runs thirty-five euros in high season and twenty-five in the low season for a visitor from outside Europe. That is more than four times the price, for a palace that is both smaller and younger. The Forbidden City is the largest preserved wooden palace complex on earth, and it is one of the best-value heritage tickets you will find anywhere. From November the first, both drop for the season. The Forbidden City to forty yuan, Versailles to twenty-five.

Inside, the route runs only one way. Since July two thousand eleven, the Palace Museum has been strictly south to north. You enter through the Meridian Gate at the south. That is the only entrance. And you leave at the north, through the Gate of Divine Prowess, or out the east side, through the East Prosperity Gate. You cannot double back, and you cannot re-enter, so pace yourself and do not rush the first courtyards. One practical catch: taxis and ride-hailing cars cannot stop right at the north gate, so plan your pickup a short walk away, or take the subway.

The building everyone comes for is the Hall of Supreme Harmony, the great throne hall on the central axis. Here is something most visitors miss. The hall you are looking at is not the original. After a series of lightning fires, it was rebuilt under the Kangxi emperor, between sixteen ninety-five and sixteen ninety-seven. At about two thousand three hundred and seventy-seven square meters, eleven bays wide and five deep, it is the largest hall in the complex, and one of the largest surviving timber structures in China. And look up at the corner of the roof. Little animal figures march along each ridge, and their number signals the building's rank. The Hall of Supreme Harmony is the only building in all of China that carries ten of them. The tenth figure appears nowhere else. Count them.

You will also hear a famous number: that the Forbidden City has nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine and a half rooms. It is a lovely story, supposedly one half-room short of the heavenly ideal of ten thousand, but it is folklore. Chinese buildings count space in jian, the bay between four pillars, and careful surveys put the real figure at eight thousand eight hundred and eighty-six bays, across roughly nine hundred and eighty surviving buildings. However you define a room, it is fewer than nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine by every count. The palace was built between fourteen oh six and fourteen twenty, and it is still the largest number of rooms you will ever walk past in a single afternoon.

One last tip for when you exit the north gate. Directly across the street is Jingshan Park, built on an artificial hill made from the earth that was dug out for the palace moat. A short, steep climb to the pavilion at the top gives you the postcard view: the entire Forbidden City laid out below you, a sea of golden roofs, with modern Beijing rising behind it. Park entry is just a couple of yuan, by most travel guides. After hours of walking at ground level, it is the view that ties the whole place together.

So, to recap. Book online, seven days ahead, with your passport. Go on any day but Monday. Enter at the Meridian Gate in the south, and climb Jingshan on your way out. Sixty yuan, soon forty, for the greatest palace in China. The only real question is how you would spend your extra time: the Treasure Gallery add-on inside, or the Jingshan view looking back?
