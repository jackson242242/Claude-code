Let's start with the simplest truth. If you only remember one thing: the two best windows to visit China are spring, roughly April into May, and autumn, September into October. In both, most of the country sits in that comfortable fifteen-to-twenty-five degree range — that's about sixty to seventy-seven Fahrenheit — with clear skies and manageable crowds in the big hubs like Beijing, Shanghai and Xi'an. Autumn gets a slight edge for scenery, because late October is when the Great Wall's hills turn gold and the mountain forests light up.

But those two windows hide a trap, and it's the most important thing in this whole video. Inside that golden autumn sits the single worst week to be in China: Golden Week, the national holiday from October first to seventh. Hundreds of millions of people travel at once. Hotels double or triple their rates. Famous sights like the Great Wall hit capacity by nine in the morning. And high-speed train tickets can sell out within seconds of release. If your dates are flexible, do not land in China for that first week of October. Shift a week later, and you get the same gold foliage with a fraction of the chaos.

Now let's walk the calendar properly, season by season, so you can slot your own trip in.

Spring. March is still cool and shoulder-season — fewer people, lower prices, the south starting to bloom. April and May are the sweet spot: warm, dry-ish, green, and gorgeous. The one date to watch is the Labour Day holiday, around May first to fifth, which spikes domestic travel for a few days. Book your trains and headline attractions before that window, and you're fine.

Summer, June through August. This is the honest weak spot. Expect real heat and heavy humidity across most of the country, plus a typhoon risk along the southeast coast. It's also peak domestic travel season, because that's when Chinese families take their holidays. So prices are up and sights are packed. But — and this is the expert move — summer is exactly when you escape upward. Travelers consistently report that the high country shines in summer: Yunnan in the southwest, with its cool highland towns, and Qinghai up on the Tibetan plateau, where the grasslands and lakes are at their best and the air stays fresh while the lowland cities bake. If you must travel in summer, go high.

Autumn, September and October. This is, for most people, the best overall season. September is warm and settling down; late October brings the foliage. The two dates to plan around: the Mid-Autumn Festival, which in 2026 falls around September twenty-fifth to twenty-seventh, and then Golden Week, October first to seventh. In some years those two holidays sit close enough to merge into one long travel surge, so treat that whole late-September-into-early-October stretch as crowded and priced-up, and aim for the back half of October if you want calm.

Winter, November through February. Here's the window almost nobody books — and that's the opportunity. From November on, flights and hotels drop a lot, and major attractions empty out. January is the coldest month, with northern China well below freezing and central China damp and chilly. But cold unlocks things you can't get any other time: Harbin's famous ice and snow festival in the northeast, snow on the Great Wall, and steaming hot-spring towns at their best. The catch in winter is Chinese New Year. In 2026 it lands on February seventeenth, with a public holiday running roughly February fifteenth through twenty-third. During that week, hundreds of millions of people travel home — trains and flights are brutal to book, and many small shops and family-run restaurants close. Early-to-mid February, before the holiday, is a quiet, cheap, beautiful time to be in China. The holiday week itself is one to plan carefully around.

A quick note on those holiday dates, because it matters for booking. China's official public-holiday calendar for the year is set by the State Council, usually announced a couple of months ahead. The 2026 dates I've given — New Year on February seventeenth, Labour Day in early May, Mid-Autumn in late September, and Golden Week October first to seventh — line up with the standard pattern, but before you lock in non-refundable flights, confirm them against the official schedule. That's the kind of thing that moves by a day or two.

So let's pull it together into a simple ranking — my honest take on when to go.

Number three: late October. The foliage is peaking, Golden Week is over, the weather's still kind. If you want the postcard version of China, this is it — just make sure you're arriving after the seventh.

Number two: April into May. Spring's at its best, everything's green and blooming, and you're well clear of the summer heat and the summer crowds. Dodge the first few days of May and it's close to perfect.

And number one, the one that surprises people: early-to-mid November, or early February before New Year. These are the shoulder weeks. You trade a little warmth for dramatically lower prices, short lines at sights that are mobbed the rest of the year, and a version of China most tourists never see. If your priority is value and space over a tan, this is the smartest time to go.

Which brings me to the bonus tip I promised. Whenever you travel, there's one move that beats the crowds even during a holiday week: spend the busiest days somewhere less famous. When everyone floods the Great Wall and the Forbidden City on October second, that's the day to be in a small canal town, a quiet mountain, or a second-tier city — then hit the headline sights on a normal weekday once the holiday ends. Crowds in China are incredibly predictable. Once you can read the calendar, you can simply step around them.
